# Human side, not AI side (company, product and landing pages)

A page can be tidy and still read as made by a model. These lines say what the person-made version does instead. They apply to a landing, home, marketing, product or company page; they replace the "one accent colour" restraint for such a page.

## The AI side: what to avoid

- Soft pastel on everything, so no colour is the brand's.
- 3D stock props, glass blobs, gradient orbs or floating icon tiles standing in for the product.
- Every section the same weight: a heading, a line, then a grid of three or four equal cards, again and again.
- Small, light, low-contrast type; a timid headline in a sans at 40px.
- Features told as icon plus two words in rounded cards; the product itself never shown.
- A round of stat tiles or testimonials with no source.

## The human side: what to do

1. **One bold brand colour and one accent**, in big colour blocks: a full-width dark or saturated band, not a tint behind a card. Two saturated hues in total; everything else is neutral, with data colours drawn from those two.
2. **Rhythm between sections**: dark hero, a light statement section, a colour block, a dark row, in an order that changes. Never more than two card-grid sections in a row; between them put a statement, a split, a product screen or a band.
3. **A headline with scale**: an editorial or display face, 56px or more on desktop, short and specific, set tight, with one idea. Body text 16px or more at a contrast of 4.5:1 or better.
4. **The real product as data**: a score, bars, rings, a table or a feed drawn as the product would show it, with believable values, large enough to read. Not an icon grid that describes it.
5. **People over props** where people matter: a photograph slot (a captioned image slot or a figure) of a real person in use, cropped with confidence, never a 3D render.
6. **Trust near the top**: a logo strip, a stat or a quote, only as the facts rule allows (below).
7. **Voice**: copy a person would say, with the audience and the one concrete benefit in it; no "unlock", "seamless", "revolutionise".

## The nine-point hero (a product or SaaS landing page)

The hero is the first screen. Check it point by point:

1. **Logo**: always present, bigger than the nav links, clickable (an `<a>`), in the page's brand style.
2. **Nav**: shows the page the visitor is on (current link marked); five or six items at most, more grouped into sections or a menu.
3. **Secondary CTA**: smaller or lighter than the main button, but the same family (same shape and type), typically log in or see how it works.
4. **Pill**: a small indicator pill above the title carrying one extra fact: a number, an update, an integration.
5. **Title**: centred, 5 to 8 words, specific to this product, not generic ("Run your books together", not "The future of finance").
6. **Subtext**: one or two lines saying the main features and who it is built for.
7. **One or two CTAs**: exactly one filled primary, and its label names the first step ("Start your first ledger"), never "Get started" alone; plus at most one secondary.
8. **Logo carousel**: only with real client names from the brief; with none, leave the strip out or label it a sample.
9. **Product screen**: the main screen of the product, drawn as a real interface with data, under the CTAs, large enough to read at 1280px and stacked, still readable, at 390px.

## Facts

Logos, customer names, quotes, ratings, user counts and results appear only when the brief or its source gives them; otherwise write them as a visible sample ("Sample quote", "Sample logos"). A product screen's own data is made up as the product would show it; a claim about the product is not.

## Checked by the host

Chrome draws the page and the host measures, in this order, what it can: two hues at most, exactly one filled primary button in the hero, a pill above the title, a 5 to 8 word centred title, a hero picture or product screen, at most two card-grid sections in a row and at least one full-width colour or dark band. The rest (the AI-side tells, the voice, the product screen's realism) is judged by the critic.
